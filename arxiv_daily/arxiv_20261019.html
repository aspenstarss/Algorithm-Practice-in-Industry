This study investigates Jev, a System One model designed for fast, structured decision-making, in information retrieval. We use Jev-as-a-judge to generate relevance judgments for four Brazilian Portuguese IR collections covering different domains and compare them with the official relevance judgments of each collection. We also evaluate Jev as a reranker. For relevance assessment, exact agreement ranged from 52.9% to 64.9%, while quadratic weighted Cohen's kappa ranged from 0.52 to 0.82. Most disagreements involved adjacent relevance levels, with at least 94.5% of annotations matching exactly or differing by only one relevance level. Despite differences at the individual query--document level, Jev-generated qrels produced similar retrieval system rankings, with Kendall's tau ranging from 0.90 to 0.93 for NormasTCU and Ulysses-RFCorpus across the two evaluation metrics. For reranking, Jev reordered the top 1,000 BM25 candidates for each of the 50 Quati test queries, increasing nDCG@10 from 0.3329 to 0.6331 (+90.2%) and MRR from 0.6852 to 0.9033 (+31.8%). These findings support Jev as a relevance assessor and show improved ranking effectiveness over BM25 in the evaluated reranking setting.

Current learned sparse approaches for recommender systems, such as STAR, represent a user by concatenating their interaction history into a single query string. For news, this creates two bottlenecks: concatenation collapses a user's distinct reading interests into one flat query, and, because news articles are long-form text, concatenating several news articles quickly overruns the context window of the language model used as backbone and forces quality-degrading truncation. To address both bottlenecks, we introduce learned sparse news recommendations (LSNR). LSNR encodes each article independently and constructs a sparse user vector by combining learned, attention-based weighting of the per-article sparse representations with a dense-projected expansion at the user level. Experiments on the MIND and EBNeRD datasets show that LSNR significantly outperforms STAR, with these gains holding even in short-history settings where truncation is not a factor. Compared to state-of-the-art dense recommenders, LSNR is competitive in standard settings and it outperforms them when the user history is short. An ablation of the user encoder confirms that learned attention-based aggregation outperforms simpler pooling alternatives. Beyond effectiveness, an additional advantage of LSNR is that it serves from an index an order of magnitude smaller than an uncompressed dense baseline.

Motivated by applications in reinforcement learning from human feedback, we consider a setting where a given collection of rankings needs to be compressed into a smaller collection of rankings. This can also be seen as a combination of two fundamental social choice frameworks: ranking aggregation and multiwinner voting. We propose three proportionality notions that capture different types of representation in this setting: positional proportionality, pairwise proportionality, and proportionality for solid coalitions (PSC). On the one hand, we show that positional proportionality and PSC are always satisfiable for any input rankings and target compression size, and a desired output can be found in polynomial time. On the other hand, we prove that pairwise proportionality cannot be satisfied in general, but can nevertheless be attained when the input rankings are single-peaked or single-crossing.

Reasoning rerankers write a rationale before ranking a candidate slate, and existing systems read item metadata to do so, even though generative recommenders increasingly represent items as compact Semantic IDs (SIDs). Ranking from SIDs alone has two appeals: SID prompts are far shorter than the matched item text, and although item codes are fixed from content, the backbone learns their token embeddings in part from user-item interaction sequences, so the tokens can carry behavioral signal that item text lacks. That training targets next-item generation, and whether it supports ranking a supplied slate from SIDs alone is untested. We build ReRankID, a controlled testbed in which models reason and rank from a history and slate shown only as SIDs, varying item rendering, history length, and slate size one at a time. On this testbed, such models often copy the input order instead of reranking, and readable metadata does not repair the failure. We compare supervised fine-tuning, rejection-sampled self-training, on-policy distillation, and verifier-based reinforcement learning as post-training methods and find that they elicit the missing behavior. The strongest post-trained model, the 8B model trained with a graded ranking reward, gives the best SID-only ranking and matches strong text-reading LLMs whose prompts are about 20x longer. It ranks above random with no history at all, where a caption-reading reference does not, and it keeps its gains at long histories, where text prompts pass 50k tokens and the caption-reading reference stops improving. In a paired rationale audit, this model argues trade-offs better than that reference but describes items less accurately. The common post-training methods we test thus elicit ranking from SIDs but not faithful item descriptions, which calls for post-training that improves the reasoning itself, not only the ranking it produces.

Open-ended search combined with foundation models has produced advances such as faster algorithms, new results on open mathematical problems, and self-improving agents. We extend this paradigm to recommendation for decision-making, focusing on decisions where the set of possible choices is fixed but the reasons for choosing them are not. In high-stakes decisions, e.g., which company to acquire or which customer to target, comparing diverse, well-reasoned options can be more useful than a single ranked list. We formalize open-ended recommendation as building a diverse, high-quality set of recommendations, each an item together with a hypothesis, a natural-language reason why the item fits the needs of a decision maker. A new recommendation enters the archive if it is novel or better than its nearest neighbor. Across M&A, customer targeting, and paper and book recommendation, QD-Rec gives the highest mean quality and builds the largest archive in most settings.

Recent studies have shown that position bias remains a critical vulnerability in large language models (LLMs), undermining both the integrity of multiple-choice evaluations and the reliability of retrieval-augmented generation. However, existing understanding of this phenomenon is largely derived from studies with limited model coverage and English-centric benchmarks, leaving the behavior of frontier models and diverse languages underexplored. In this work, we conduct a comprehensive investigation of position bias across model scales ranging from billion-level to trillion-level parameters, covering both multiple-choice and multi-document tasks in five languages. User simulators enable interactive training and evaluation of AI assistants at scale. Yet current simulators struggle to reproduce the diverse behaviors of real users, leading to assistants that generalize poorly to underrepresented users. We identify user representations as an underexplored bottleneck in user simulation. User representations are natural-language descriptions that condition simulators to reproduce a particular user's behavior. A representation's quality becomes apparent in hindsight, through the behavior it induces in a simulator. To evaluate and optimize user representations, we formulate a conversation reconstruction task: given an original conversation, we generate a representation, condition a simulator on it, simulate a conversation, and then measure fidelity by comparing user behavior in the original and simulated conversations. This comparison also yields hindsight feedback, which identifies where the representation falls short and how it can be improved. We introduce hindsight distillation, an online training procedure that distills feedback-conditioned improvements into the representation generator, enabling it to generate improved representations directly from the original conversation. We present UserRepGen-4B, a representation generator trained with hindsight distillation. Across 700 conversations spanning seven datasets, hindsight feedback helps improve user representations at test time, and UserRepGen-4B generates representations that enable more faithful simulation than those generated by GPT-5.5. Beyond conversation reconstruction, learned representations improve simulation fidelity for unseen tasks, better preserve the population-level diversity of real users, and better distinguish users across conversations.

Long-context decoding in large language models (LLMs) is increasingly bottlenecked by the memory and computation required to attend over an ever-growing Key-Value (KV) cache at each autoregressive step. Although token-level sparse attention reduces this burden by restricting computation to a small subset of relevant past tokens, its practical gains are often offset by repeatedly recomputing layer-wise sparse indices as the context grows. A common remedy is to share sparse indices across layers by computing them only at designated anchor layers, but such static reuse ignores the layer-wise evolution of attention patterns, causing the reused indices to progressively deviate from the layer-specific optima and incur substantial attention loss. By revisiting cross-layer sparsity from the perspective of attention dynamics, we find that inter-layer salient token shift is highly localized: although the exact top-$κ$ indices differ across layers, the core high-attention tokens remain stable, with changes concentrated near the expanded boundary of previously selected regions. Motivated by this, we propose Prism Attention, a proposal-refined sparse attention framework. It replaces rigid cross-layer index reuse with a coarse-to-fine strategy, preserving layer-wise adaptability while retaining the efficiency benefits of cross-layer sharing. Extensive experiments show that Prism Attention achieves state-of-the-art performance among mainstream sparse attention methods on both reasoning and long-context benchmarks, while attaining a $3.4\times$ end-to-end decoding speedup over FlashAttention at 128K context length.

Optimizing product listings for generative search requires language agents not only to rewrite effectively but also to continually self-improve by retaining successful strategies across diverse categories. However, existing approaches often treat structured listings as free-form text, leaving effective strategies implicit in prompts, search trajectories, or rewritten outputs rather than accumulating them as a reusable agentic memory. To address this limitation, we introduce GLOBE, an agentic framework that drives continual self-improvement by storing optimization knowledge in an evolving handbook of explicit, field-scoped behaviors. In each episode, an Editor applies relevant behaviors and receives rank-based feedback, which a Reflector converts into behavior updates. Crucially for dependable execution in real-world settings, a Moderator removes redundant proposals, a Curator commits accepted changes, and consistently harmful behaviors are deactivated. This cycle transforms episodic feedback into a persistent, field-scoped optimization memory. Experiments show that structured behaviors outperform monolithic prompt optimization, while feedback-driven evolution and moderation further improve effectiveness and reduce downside risk. The learned behaviors also remain effective on unseen product categories, suggesting that agentic memory evolution supports interpretable and transferable product optimization.
