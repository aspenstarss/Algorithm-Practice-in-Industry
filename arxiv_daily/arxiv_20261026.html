System One models, or decision models, answer a question about an input with a calibrated probability instead of generated text. Within weeks of the first release, in September 2026, they were used as rerankers. We test whether the decision fine-tuning behind them makes a better reranker than plain reranker fine-tuning of the same model. For six Qwen backbones from 0.6B to 27B parameters and ten TREC DL and BEIR collections, we compare the untuned model, asked zero-shot, with its decision fine-tunes and its reranker fine-tunes, two of which we train ourselves. In our work, we draw the conclusion that decision fine-tuning never makes the better reranker. From 0.6B to 9B, reranker fine-tuning wins on every backbone by 5 to 29 points nDCG@10, also on zero-shot collections, and at 27B the two tie. Up to 9B, decision fine-tuning adds nothing over simply asking the untuned model. Moreover, we find that the calibration advantage of decision models depends on the question. Asked whether a passage answers the query, they are better calibrated than the published reranker fine-tunes from 4B on. Asked a slightly different question from prior work, they lose this advantage. Lastly, calibration does not make up for a worse ranking: up to 9B, a reranker fine-tune keeps the better passages at a fixed threshold. If you need a reranker, fine-tune a reranker.

Emerging e-commerce platforms, such as live-streaming and Online-to-Offline (O2O) services, inherently operate under a "Container-based" display paradigm. In this setting, a critical granularity mismatch exists: the platform performs ranking and estimation at the container level (e.g., livestream rooms or stores), whereas the actual transactions occur at the item level within these containers. This structural discrepancy complicates Gross Transaction Value (GTV) estimation, introducing unique challenges including high label variance, severe label sparsity, and dynamic item heterogeneity. To address these challenges, we propose a novel framework named Hierarchical GTV Estimation (HGE). HGE employs a Hybrid Set-Aware Encoder (HSAE) to model the dynamic composition of items and feature interactions. To mitigate label variance, we introduce a Proxy-Label Learning (PLL) strategy that decomposes post-click GTV into purchase probability, quantity, and unit price. Furthermore, a Residual Correction and Ensemble Fusion (RCEF) module is designed to handle label sparsity by effectively combining item-level aggregation with container-level inference. Extensive experiments on large-scale industrial datasets demonstrate that HGE significantly outperforms state-of-the-art baselines, achieving a +0.63% lift in XAUC offline and a +1.42% increase in Revenue Per Search (RPS) in online A/B tests. HGE framework has been fully deployed into our main traffic.

Designing optimal auctions for allocating public infrastructure-such as airport slots and energy resources-is challenging due to interdependent resources and complex side-constraints. While the Vickrey-Clarke-Groves (VCG) auction guarantees maximum welfare, it cannot maximize revenue or accommodate the multi-objective trade-offs inherent in public infrastructure systems. We propose a differentiable, constraint-aware framework for combinatorial auction design that enables end-to-end learning of fractionally feasible, approximately strategy-proof mechanisms. Our primary contribution is a combinatorial constraint enforcement technique that ensures fractional feasibility by construction, bypassing the need for penalty-based approximations. We introduce CAGraph, a novel graph-based architecture designed to capture dependencies between items and bidders, alongside CANet and CAFormer as deep learning baselines, for combinatorial settings. We also provide a Matching Pursuit-inspired decomposition that maps fractional allocations to feasible discrete allocations and empirically evaluates the resulting discretization gap. In synthetic benchmarks and an airport slot-divestiture case study, respectively, the learned mechanisms achieve up to 73.5% and 180% higher expected revenue than VCG, under small empirical regret. Finally, we visualize the Pareto frontiers for revenue, welfare, and fairness, providing regulators with a decision-support tool to design infrastructure auctions that balance profitability, efficiency and equity.

Late-interaction models offer state-of-the-art efficiency-effectiveness trade-offs for neural retrieval by enabling document representations to be pre-computed and efficiently indexed. Yet, their relatively simple interaction mechanisms, typically MaxSim, can limit their effectiveness compared to cross-encoders. MICE, a recently introduced re-ranking architecture, aims to combine cross-encoder-like effectiveness through expressive transformer-based scoring with reduced inference-time computation through document-side pre-computation. However, no practical way to store these document representations was explored. We address this limitation here and introduce MICE-P, the first end-to-end retrieval pipeline with MICE, based on PLAID to index the contextualized document token representations. Generative recommenders using semantic identifiers predict items through successive token decisions, but beam search may irreversibly prune promising item paths. This limitation is particularly consequential when a few recent interactions signal an emerging interest while earlier behavior favors established preferences. Yet an independently trained discriminative recommender may rank the same items highly from the same interaction history. Such complementary scores are available at test time, offering a way to adapt a frozen generator without retraining either model. The challenge is to use these scores before pruning: combining completed recommendation lists cannot alter earlier beam decisions, while scores over complete items cannot directly inform choices among semantic prefixes shared by multiple items. We propose \textbf{T}est-Time \textbf{C}ollaborative \textbf{B}eam \textbf{S}earch \textbf{A}daptation (\textbf{TCBSA}), a training-free method that incorporates collaborative information into semantic decoding. TCBSA transforms discriminative score margins into potentials over shared semantic edges and uses a confidence gate to regulate their contributions to cumulative path scores before beam selection. We establish a sufficient condition for target prefix survival under global beam competition and bound score perturbations under low discriminative confidence. Full-catalog experiments show consistent improvements over strong baselines across datasets and different generative and discriminative recommenders. Analyses of emerging interests and decoding behavior demonstrate gains in recommendation accuracy and target path survival, while targeted interventions support the contribution of shared prefix corrections. TCBSA also offers a favorable balance between recommendation accuracy and inference latency compared with simply widening the native beam.

Generative recommendation with semantic IDs represents each item as a sequence of discrete codes derived from its semantic representation and predicts these codes to generate recommendations. Existing semantic-ID recommenders are typically trained with hard labels for the observed next item. Although such supervision identifies the target, it provides no explicit distributional information about alternative items conditioned on the same user history. Sequential recommenders can supply this item-level predictive knowledge, but transferring it to semantic-ID generation is challenging because the teacher and student operate in item and code spaces, respectively. We propose two complementary knowledge-distillation objectives for parallel semantic-ID recommenders, which predict all code positions simultaneously without autoregressive prefix conditioning. Item Distribution Matching (IDM) reconstructs a catalogue-level item distribution from the student's parallel code predictions and aligns it with the teacher's item distribution. Code Marginal Matching (CMM) projects the teacher's item probabilities onto each code position using fixed item-to-code assignments, then matches the resulting code marginals to the corresponding student heads. Both objectives transfer the teacher's soft distributional knowledge while preserving the student's parallel prediction structure. Experiments on benchmark datasets show that both methods consistently outperform existing state-of-the-art approaches. On average, IDM improves Recall@10 by 15.39%, while CMM improves NDCG@10 by 8.28%. These results demonstrate that distillation in either reconstructed item space or code-marginal space can substantially strengthen hard-label supervision for parallel generative recommendation.

Generative recommenders retrieve items by writing their semantic IDs (SIDs) code by code under trie-constrained beam search, an approach now deployed on large web platforms. Standard autoregressive interfaces pay for the SID length K twice: a history of T items enters the backbone as TK code tokens, and every beam-search step runs through the backbone, so returning B candidates costs O(BK) backbone decode tokens per user. The second cost often dominates, and it grows with the backbone, the SID depth and the candidate budget. We introduce Deco (decoding from concepts), which separates reading the history from writing the identifier. The backbone reads each history item as one fused token and condenses the history once into a compact next-item concept; a light KV-cached decoder conditioned on the concept and the generated prefix runs the entire beam search, while the identifiers, the trie and the autoregressive factorization stay unchanged. The decoder approaches full-backbone accuracy with cross-entropy alone, and distillation improves it further. On seven datasets from e-commerce, movies and music streaming, Deco achieves comparable or better accuracy than both a standard generative recommender and the full-backbone teacher with fused inputs, while delivering 3.2--8.6$\times$ their throughput under a 50 ms p95 latency budget in a production-style serving pipeline. Delegating beam search makes larger candidate pools and models practical: Deco generates 1000 candidates faster than its teacher generates 100, and with a 1.5B-parameter LLM backbone, it matches the 69M-parameter teacher's throughput while achieving higher accuracy.
